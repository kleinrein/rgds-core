# Plan: fork Tanuki3DS and make 3DS games faster

The fork exists (2026-11-02): [kleinrein/Tanuki3DS-rgds](https://github.com/kleinrein/Tanuki3DS-rgds), branch `rgds`, and `tools/tanuki3ds` builds from it.

Second pass, 2026-11-02/03 (installed on the handheld 2026-11-03 01:35 as the engine's `ctremu` with the new `run.sh`, a combined binary with the save-state format that survives rebuilds is the save persistence session's to build and install; the previous ones kept beside them as `ctremu.installed-1102`, `ctremu.installed-1103` and `run.sh.installed-1102`): a present thread so the renderer no longer waits for the two panels' vblanks (NSMB2 levels: renderer 16 to 4-6 ms a frame, nothing lost); the coin sprites drew wrong because gapped texture copies marked too short a range and the game also writes textures with no flush, so the whole texture is compared once a frame; Mario Kart races are bound by draw cost on both sides (renderer thread about 8 ms of CPU a frame with no draws at all, the GPU about 7 ms more of waiting, lit draws a quarter of the draws and 40% of the GPU wait). Sampling the renderer thread showed which Mesa really loads: through the launcher it was our build with glthread (the `lib3` gallium next to `lib2`'s EGL), by hand it was Ubuntu's; `lib2` held a day-older build without the glthread cap. `lib2` now carries the right build and `run.sh` puts it first; the renderer thread's own CPU work in a race is about 11% our code, the rest the driver, which glthread moves to a fourth core. A one-pixel scissor (no fragment work) lifted a race only from 62% to 74% of frames, so fragments are not the main limit. With glthread the driver thread (`ctremu:gl0`) became the limit at about 70% of a core; a GL state cache on the draw path cut the calls per draw from 39 to 7.6: Mario Kart races 88% to 96.5% drawn, no gap over 40 ms, NSMB2 map 46 to 60 fps, levels 99.7%. Internal resolution (2026-11-03, per game on the Graphics page, taken at the next launch): 3D Land at 2x keeps its 30 fps with cleaner edges; NSMB2 at 2x drops to 83% of frames in levels and 62% on the map, so it stays at 1x, as does Mario Kart 7. Sleeping waits (2026-11-03) changed neither the frame rate nor the heat curve (56 to 68 °C in the first 90 s of a race either way): the heat is the emulation's real work. What is lost in a race now falls in the start pack (all karts in view, about 410 draws a frame against 380 later; from the second lap on nothing is lost). The main thread there is at 80-84% of its core: 57% guest code from the JIT, 7% the JIT dispatcher, 6% block lookup (`get_jitblock`), 3% walking command lists; the chip reaches its 70 °C trip after about 90 s of racing and the CPU drops to 1.6 GHz, which costs frames by itself.

Third pass, 2026-11-03 afternoon ("still not 60 stable with Performance on"): the owner's 85-minute Mario Kart log showed game speed at 60 throughout and the renderer thread saturated at 16.5 ms a frame, 98.5% of frames drawn in the first ten minutes and 86-88% after minute twenty, because the chip reaches the 80 °C trip after about ten minutes of racing (three busy cores and the GPU at 800 MHz) and the CPU is then held at 1416-1608 MHz for the rest. At that clock the renderer thread and Mesa's driver thread, each about half a core at 1992 MHz, no longer fit a frame. Sampling the driver thread (`libgallium` with symbols) showed a seventh of it scanning every draw's indices for the vertex range: Mesa's glthread drops the range `glDrawRangeElementsBaseVertex` gives it, and Panfrost needs the range for its vertex job. A Mesa patch (in `build-mesa.sh`) keeps the range: a race at the same heat went from 98.2-98.6% to 99.5% of frames drawn, the driver thread from 51% to 39% of a core; skipping the client wait on the present set's fence on top gave 99.8% and 34%, and in the owner's hands far more: through the launcher that wait costs 5.5 ms a frame, and their own runs drew 78% of frames with it against 97.5% without, so it is off by default. The blinking reported that morning with either build was always another launch on the panels at the same time (a test run, another session's game or benchmark). Ten minutes of Grand Prix with both, the chip at its trip from minute four (CPU 1416-1608 MHz, GPU 600 MHz, a harsher state than the owner's Performance mode): 99.6% of frames drawn, 99.2-99.8% in every two-minute slice after the first, where the owner's log had 86-88%. Installed on the handheld 2026-11-04 morning on the owner's OK (engine `ctremu` = the no-wait build, Mesa `lib2` = the range patch; the owner's verdict on a quiet handheld: "much better"), after a state check both ways (each engine loads the other's Mario Kart state, every chunk written back identical); the previous engine and library are kept beside them as `ctremu.installed-1104-0845` and `lib2/libgallium-25.2.8.so.pre-range`. The "Internal resolution" row never worked because the game menu's request reader dropped `play-scale:` unread; fixed with a test through the request file and proved end to end on a copy of the owner's data (the OS image installed that afternoon carries it). Also in the engine: vertex buffers laid out for the driver on the main thread (no effect in Mario Kart, whose layouts are all floats and bytes), a hashed texture lookup instead of a 256-entry list scan per texture per draw, the internal scale and each distinct vertex layout in the run log. GPU time per frame in a race at 800 MHz: 12.5 ms mean, 14 at the 90th percentile, so the GPU is the tightest resource at full clocks and the thermal budget decides the rest.

Speed, first pass (2026-11-02, installed on the handheld as the engine's `ctremu`, the build before it kept as `ctremu.keep`): the owner's NSMB2 hand play rebuilt a 1024x1024 ETC1A4 atlas 10-16 times a half second, 23 ms each, 116 s of a 20-minute run, because texture copies that were not whole atlas rows dropped the texture and decoded it whole. ETC textures now keep a copy of their bytes and upload only the 8x8 tiles that changed. Same World 1-1 route, old build then new: full ETC1A4 rebuilds 2481 to 67 (all first sightings), texture time 55.5 s to 0.2 s, frames drawn in the level 17-20 of 30 to 27-30, frames lost over the route 2780 to 415. Pictures checked against the old build's screenshots. Every run's numbers now stay in `logs/` in the game's home.

**A fork adds no speed.** The speed work below is what fixes the lag.

- Size of our changes: about 6,100 lines across 32 files, one commit on the `rgds` branch.
- Upstream: our base commit `879a1e7` is still its latest, and recent commits there are mostly build and CI changes, so there is little to merge.
- Licence: Tanuki3DS is GPL-3, so the source has to be offered if we ship the engine anyway. A public fork is the simplest way to do that.

## Part 1: Speed

Best lead: the owner's 30-40 fps may match a stretch in the last scripted NSMB2 run. There it shows 19-20 pictures per half second (38-40 fps) instead of 31, because about 12 textures per half second are rebuilt at about 23 ms each, which takes about half of all the time. It is not confirmed that this was the scene being played.

The handheld does not look like the limit. During a hand-played NSMB2 session the CPU and GPU were at full clock, the temperature was 64 °C, and the two busiest threads were at 53% and 71%.

If that is not enough, decide per game whether it is worth keeping. Azahar is not a likely rescue: the estimate for it was 30-35 fps at best, and that would need a risky rewrite.

Splitting the one commit into renderer, CPU, save states, menu and sound pieces was dropped: a day of work that fixes no lag. Bisect by the speed commits that follow it instead.

## Things to know first

- Any new engine build makes existing 3DS save states and auto-saves unusable; in-game saves are safe. Most speed fixes need a new build, so save in-game before each one. Making states survive updates would be a separate piece of work.
- Recommended order: speed first, because that is what hurts and the fork can wait. The downside is that the speed fixes pile onto the patch file until then.
